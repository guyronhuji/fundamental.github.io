> **The short answer.** Epigenetics is the set of chemical marks that tell a cell which of its genes to use. Every cell in your body carries the same DNA; epigenetic marks are why a nerve cell and a skin cell read that DNA so differently.

## One instruction book, many kinds of cell

Almost every cell in your body holds the same DNA, a complete copy of the instructions for building a person. Yet a heart cell, a neuron and a white blood cell look and behave nothing alike. The difference is not in the instructions but in which pages each cell reads.

Epigenetics is the system of bookmarks and paper clips. Chemical tags attached to the DNA, and to the proteins it is wrapped around, mark some genes as "use this" and others as "keep closed". The DNA text stays the same; what changes is how it is read.

## Where the marks sit

Inside a cell, DNA is not a loose thread. It is wound around protein spools called histones, like thread on millions of tiny bobbins. Tags on these spools control how tightly the DNA is packed. Loosely packed genes can be read; tightly packed genes are silent.

Unlike the DNA sequence, these marks can change during life. They shift as an embryo develops, as cells specialise, and in response to signals from their surroundings.

## What this has to do with cancer

Cancer is usually described as a disease of mutations, errors in the DNA text. That is true, but it is half the story. Tumour cells also rearrange their epigenetic marks, switching on genes that drive growth and silencing genes that would hold it back.

Two things follow. First, cells within one tumour can carry different marks and behave differently, even with the same mutations. A small group of them may be far more aggressive than the rest. Second, because the marks record what kind of cell a fragment came from and what state it was in, they can serve as a fingerprint of the tumour.

## What our group does

We develop computational methods to read these marks cell by cell and molecule by molecule, in collaboration with the Shema Lab at the Weizmann Institute. Measuring the marks in single cells lets us find small groups of cells that an average over the whole tumour would hide.

In our 2026 *Nature* paper, this is how we found a small population of aggressive cells inside breast tumours of a type usually considered less aggressive, and then detected the same epigenetic signature in patients' blood.
