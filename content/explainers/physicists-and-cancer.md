> **The short answer.** Modern biology produces enormous amounts of noisy measurement data, and finding a faint, real signal in noisy data is what experimental physicists are trained to do. The questions belong to biology; many of the tools are shared.

## A fair question

Our group measures the decay of radioactive atoms and the size of the proton. We also work on breast cancer and lymphoma. People reasonably ask what one has to do with the other.

The answer is not that physicists understand cancer better than biologists. We don't. It is that the bottleneck in parts of biology has moved from making measurements to making sense of them.

## Biology became a data problem

A generation ago, a tumour was studied as one sample giving one averaged answer. Today a single experiment can measure dozens of properties in each of millions of individual cells, or read chemical marks on single molecules in a drop of blood.

That creates a familiar problem. The interesting thing is often rare: a few unusual cells among millions of ordinary ones, or a handful of tumour fragments among a vast background from healthy tissue. The instruments are imperfect, the samples vary from day to day and from hospital to hospital, and it is easy to find patterns that are not really there.

## What physics brings

Particle and nuclear physicists have spent decades on exactly this kind of problem: searching for rare events in huge datasets, where the detector itself distorts what you see. The habits that come with it carry over directly.

- **Understand the instrument.** Know how the measurement can mislead you before you trust what it says.
- **Model the background.** You cannot claim a signal until you can describe what its absence looks like.
- **Quantify uncertainty.** Every result comes with an honest estimate of how wrong it could be.
- **Be suspicious of your own result.** Check whether the effect survives a different sample, a different method, a different analyst.

## What it doesn't bring

Physics does not supply the biological questions, the experiments, or the judgement about what a result means for patients. Those come from our collaborators. This work exists because of a partnership with Efrat Shema's lab at the Weizmann Institute, whose methods produce the data, and it depends on learning enough biology to ask sensible questions of it.

## What our group does

In our 2026 *Nature* paper, we treated single-cell and single-molecule epigenetic data as an experimental physics problem. That helped reveal a small population of aggressive cells inside breast tumours, and a matching signal in patients' blood.
