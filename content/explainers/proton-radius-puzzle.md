> **The short answer.** In 2010, a new and very precise measurement found the proton to be about 4% smaller than every earlier measurement had said. The earlier ones used electrons; the new one used muons, the electron's heavier cousin. Physicists have spent the years since working out why they disagreed.

## How big is a proton?

The proton sits at the centre of every hydrogen atom and, with the neutron, makes up nearly all the ordinary matter around us. It is not a point. Its electric charge is spread over a small region, a little under one millionth of a billionth of a metre across.

You cannot hold a ruler to it, so its size is measured indirectly, in two ways. One is to fire electrons at protons and see how they bounce off. The other is to study hydrogen atoms very precisely: the electron orbiting the proton spends a little of its time inside it, which shifts the atom's energy levels by a tiny, measurable amount.

For decades the two methods agreed: a radius of about 0.88 femtometres.

## The measurement that didn't fit

In 2010, a team at the Paul Scherrer Institute in Switzerland tried a sharper version of the atomic method. They replaced the electron in hydrogen with a muon. A muon behaves like an electron but is about 200 times heavier, so it orbits far closer to the proton and feels its size much more strongly.

Their answer was 0.84 femtometres. The difference sounds small, but it was far larger than the uncertainties on either side allowed. Something was wrong somewhere.

## Why it mattered

There were two kinds of explanation. The dull one: some of the older measurements, or the way they were analysed, were slightly off. The exciting one: muons and electrons do not interact with protons in quite the same way. Our best theory of particles, the Standard Model, says they should behave identically apart from their mass. If they don't, there is a force or particle we don't know about.

## Where things stand

Several newer experiments with electrons have since given the smaller value, and most physicists now think the proton radius is close to 0.84 femtometres. But the story is not closed. It is still not fully understood why some earlier results came out larger, and one piece of evidence has been missing all along: nobody had measured the proton's size by scattering muons off it directly.

## What our group does

That is the job of MUSE, the Muon Scattering Experiment at the Paul Scherrer Institute. MUSE fires both muons and electrons, of both electric charges, at the same hydrogen target and records them in the same detectors, so the two can be compared side by side. If muons and electrons see the proton differently, MUSE is designed to show it.

Our group designed and built one of its central detectors, the Straw Tube Tracker, which records the paths of the particles scattered from the target.
