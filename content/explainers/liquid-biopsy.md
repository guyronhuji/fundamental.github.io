> **The short answer.** A liquid biopsy is a test that looks for signs of disease in a sample of body fluid, usually blood, instead of cutting out a piece of tissue. It works because cells, including tumour cells, constantly shed fragments of themselves into the bloodstream.

## Why not just take a piece of the tumour?

A standard biopsy means removing tissue with a needle or surgery. It tells doctors a great deal, but it has limits. It can be painful and risky, it can only be done once a lump has been found, and it samples one spot of a tumour that may differ from place to place. It is also hard to repeat every few months to check whether a treatment is working.

A blood draw has none of those problems. The question is whether blood carries enough information.

## What a tumour leaves in the blood

Cells die all the time, and when they do, their contents spill into the bloodstream. Among the debris are short pieces of DNA, still wound around the protein spools that package DNA inside every cell. One spool with its DNA is called a nucleosome.

These fragments carry two kinds of information. The DNA sequence can reveal mutations. The spools carry chemical tags, called epigenetic marks, that record which genes were switched on or off in the cell the fragment came from. Those tags differ between a healthy breast cell and a tumour cell, so they act as a return address.

The difficulty is quantity. In early cancer, only a tiny share of the fragments in a blood sample comes from the tumour. Finding them is a search for a faint signal in a great deal of noise.

## What a liquid biopsy can and cannot do

Some liquid biopsies are already in clinical use, mainly to choose treatments and track known cancers by their DNA mutations. Using them to detect cancer early, in people with no symptoms, is harder and still largely a research goal. A useful test must catch real cancers without raising false alarms in healthy people, and proving that takes large trials over many years.

## What our group does

Together with the Shema Lab at the Weizmann Institute, we work on a method called EPINUC, which reads the epigenetic marks on single nucleosomes in blood plasma, one molecule at a time. Our group builds the computational tools that find the tumour's pattern in that data.

In our 2026 *Nature* paper, this approach distinguished early-stage luminal breast cancer with 95% accuracy and predicted relapse within five years with 81% accuracy, in stored samples from several hospitals. It is a research result, not yet a test anyone can order: it has to be confirmed in large forward-looking trials before it could be used in the clinic.
