> **The short answer.** The Standard Model is our best theory of the particles and forces that make up the universe, and it has passed every laboratory test so far. But it leaves out things we know exist, so physicists are sure it is incomplete. "Beyond the Standard Model" is the search for what is missing.

## A theory that works too well

The Standard Model describes the basic particles of matter and three of the four forces of nature: electromagnetism, the strong force that binds atomic nuclei, and the weak force behind radioactive decay. Its predictions have been checked to extraordinary precision, and the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 confirmed its last missing piece.

Yet nobody thinks it is the full story.

## What it leaves out

- **Dark matter.** Galaxies move as if they contain several times more matter than we can see. None of the Standard Model's particles fits the description.
- **Matter over antimatter.** The early universe should have made matter and antimatter in equal amounts, which would then have destroyed each other. We are here, so something tipped the balance.
- **Neutrino masses.** The theory originally said neutrinos weigh nothing. Experiments show they have a small mass.
- **Gravity.** It is not part of the theory at all.

So there must be particles or forces we have not yet found. The question is how to find them.

## Two ways to look

The first is brute force: smash particles together at the highest energies possible, as at the Large Hadron Collider, and try to create new particles directly.

The second is precision. Even a particle too heavy to create leaves fingerprints: it very slightly changes how ordinary processes unfold. If you measure a well-understood process precisely enough and the result differs from the Standard Model's prediction, you have found evidence of something new, without ever producing it. This approach can be sensitive to physics beyond the direct reach of today's colliders, and the experiments fit in a laboratory room.

## Why beta decay

Beta decay is a form of radioactivity in which a nucleus emits an electron and a neutrino. It is driven by the weak force, and the Standard Model predicts in detail how the two particles should fly apart: at which angles and with what energies. A new force would shift those patterns by a tiny amount.

## What our group does

We measure those patterns as precisely as we can. To see a decay cleanly, the atom should be held almost still in empty space, with nothing around it to disturb the particles that come out. We do this by trapping radioactive atoms, such as neon and helium isotopes, with lasers or electric fields, and recording the decay products one event at a time. We then compare the results with Standard Model calculations at the level of about one part in a thousand.
